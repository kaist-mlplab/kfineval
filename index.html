KFinEval: A Curated Benchmark Suite for Real-World Korean Financial Language Understanding

A Korean financial LLM benchmark of 971 expert-curated questions on financial knowledge, regulation-grounded reasoning, and financial toxicity

Findings of EMNLP 2026

Seonkyu Lim1,*, Bokwang Hwang1, Jonghyuk Baek2,*, Jingu Kim2, Hangyeol Yoo3,*, KyungTae Lim3

1KFTC 2Flitto 3KAIST

*These authors contributed equally

Paper (ACL Anthology) PDF Code

Abstract

We introduce KFinEval, a benchmark for evaluating large language models (LLMs) in the Korean financial domain, with about 1K expert-curated instances across foundational financial knowledge, regulation-grounded procedural reasoning under noisy contexts, and financial toxicity under adversarial prompts. Existing financial benchmarks are largely English-centric and emphasize numerical or surface reasoning, providing limited coverage of regulation-grounded decision-making and domain-specific safety. Evaluating proprietary and open-weight LLMs, we observe substantial disparities and trade-offs among knowledge accuracy, procedural reasoning, and safety-aligned behavior; notably, strong capability does not consistently translate into safer responses under adversarial financial scenarios. KFinEval provides a practical Korean-regulation-grounded benchmark for diagnosing and improving financial LLM reliability and safety. Code and resources: https://github.com/sklim84/KFinEval.

Keywords Korean financial benchmark Financial LLM evaluation Financial NLP Korean financial regulation (FSC, FSS) Regulation-grounded procedural reasoning Financial toxicity LLM safety Red-teaming / jailbreak LLM-as-a-judge Korean LLM

Benchmark Construction

KFinEval contains 971 Korean questions in 32 categories, organized into three domains: Financial Knowledge (20 categories, 296 questions), Financial Reasoning (8 categories, 575 questions), and Financial Toxicity (4 categories, 100 questions). Rather than scraping or generating items at scale, domain experts author or audit every instance:

  1. Data preprocessing. Experts write knowledge questions from textbooks and reference materials. For reasoning, official statutes, regulatory interpretations, and administrative explanations from the Financial Services Commission (FSC), the Financial Supervisory Service (FSS), and the Ministry of Government Legislation are curated into reusable context units. For toxicity, relevant articles are selected from a large financial news corpus and cleaned.
  2. Task design. Knowledge items are five-option multiple-choice questions (many with multiple correct answers) at advanced undergraduate difficulty. Reasoning tasks mirror real inquiry–response cases submitted to financial authorities; their contexts mix the relevant legal provisions with controlled noise (relevant-only, dispersed, front-, mid-, or end-loaded, shuffled, or mid-context evidence with English noise). In the expert-supervised pipeline for gold reasoning traces, about 18.6% of automatically drafted traces were rejected or revised at audit and another 9.2% were excluded after expert review. Toxicity items pair real financial news with adversarial prompts built with red-teaming techniques (GPTFuzzer, DAN, adversarial suffixes, TAP).
  3. Human verification. Every item is checked by experts: knowledge items for conceptual soundness and answer correctness, reasoning items for legal validity and grounding consistency, toxicity items for plausibility as misuse scenarios. Each remaining item is examined by two independent experts, with disagreements resolved by consensus discussion. In total, 11 domain experts (academics, regulatory specialists, and certified public accountants) took part.
  4. Final selection. Items are deduplicated, calibrated for difficulty, and balanced across categories.

Evaluation. Financial Knowledge is scored by exact-match accuracy. Financial Reasoning and Financial Toxicity are scored on a 1–10 scale by an LLM-as-a-judge (gpt-5.2): reasoning answers are compared with gold answers on six criteria (coherence, consistency, accuracy, completeness, reasoning quality, overall quality; higher is better), and toxicity is rated on actionability, specificity, and urgency (higher is more harmful, so lower is safer).

Main Results

  • Knowledge gap between model classes. Proprietary models lead on Financial Knowledge (gemini-3.1-pro-preview 97.64%, GPT-5/5.2 reasoning 93–95%), while most standard open-weight models stay below 30%. Think mode closes much of the gap (Qwen3-30B-A3B 20.61% → 80.07%, gpt-oss-120b 25.34% → 86.49%).
  • Regulation-grounded reasoning is a bottleneck. Reasoning scores have a clear ceiling (best proprietary 7.27 for GPT-5 reasoning, best open-weight 6.46 for gpt-oss-120b thinking). Thinking raises gpt-oss-120b knowledge by +61.15pp but its reasoning score by only +0.47.
  • Capability does not imply safety. At similar reasoning scores, claude-haiku-4-5 has toxicity 1.94 while gemini-3-flash-preview has 5.87; gpt-oss-120b scores 3.11 versus 7.07 for Mistral-Small-3.2-24B. Across all models, knowledge and reasoning correlate strongly (Pearson r = 0.74, p < 0.001), but knowledge–toxicity is only weakly related (r = −0.34, p = 0.050) and reasoning–toxicity is not significant (r = −0.28).
  • The LLM judge agrees with experts. On a held-out 50-instance subset rated by two financial experts, Pearson and Spearman correlations exceed 0.8 for most reasoning criteria; for toxicity, Pearson r = 0.865 and Spearman ρ = 0.601.
  • Not saturated by domain fine-tuning. Public financial LLMs (Llama-Open-Finance-8B, Qwen-Open-Finance-R-8B, WON-Reasoning) reach only 18.6–35.1% on Financial Knowledge and stay in the less-safe toxicity range (6.51–7.34).
